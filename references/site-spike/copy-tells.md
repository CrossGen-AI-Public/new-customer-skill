# Copy tells

Adapted from [blader/humanizer](https://github.com/blader/humanizer) (MIT), which encodes
Wikipedia's ["Signs of AI writing"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)
as 35 patterns. Reorganised here for website copy, where the failures concentrate in headlines,
subheads, section intros, CTAs, and the about paragraph.

**The governing rule:** never invent a fact to make a sentence work. No name, number, date,
quote, customer, or claim goes on the page unless it came from the client. If a line needs a
detail you do not have, write a simpler line or ask for the detail.

---

## The four that ruin website copy

**Sales language (h.4).** "Transform your workflow." "Unlock your potential." "Seamlessly
integrate." This is the buzzword tell from the visual list, and it is the reason most generated
pages fail the swap test on the first line.
→ Say what the thing does, for whom, in the client's own words. "We rebuild HVAC compressors
for commercial buildings in Denver" beats any verb from the transformation family.

**Inflated importance (h.1).** "A leading provider." "Revolutionising the industry." "A testament
to our commitment." Claims of significance the client cannot support.
→ Replace with a fact. Years, count, named customer, measurable outcome. If there is no fact,
cut the sentence; do not soften it.

**Vague sources (h.5).** "Studies show." "Industry experts agree." "Trusted by teams everywhere."
→ Name the study, the expert, the teams. If you cannot name them, the claim does not go on the page.

**Generic positive endings (h.25).** The closing section that says the future is bright and the
journey continues.
→ End on the last concrete thing: the price, the next step, the phone number, the real plan.

## Sentence-level tells

| # | Tell | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| h.3 | Shallow `-ing` clauses tacked on: "...helping teams move faster." | Cut the clause or make it a real sentence with a subject. |
| h.7 | Overused AI words: *crucial, delve, enhance, foster, landscape, leverage, pivotal, robust, seamless, showcase, streamline, tapestry, testament, underscore, unlock, vibrant, empower, elevate, transform, cutting-edge, best-in-class, holistic, bespoke, curated* | Plain verb, plain noun. |
| h.8 | Avoiding *is/are*: "serves as", "stands as", "boasts", "features", "offers" | Use *is*, *are*, *has*. |
| h.9 | "Not just X, it's Y" and clipped negatives ("no guesswork", "no fluff") | Write the clause out. |
| h.10 | Forced groups of three - three benefits, three adjectives, three cards | Say the number of things there actually are. |
| h.11 | Renaming the same thing to avoid repetition; every sentence opening the same way | One name for one thing. Vary the sentence, not the noun. |
| h.12 | Fake ranges: "from startups to enterprises", "from strategy to execution" | Name the actual span, or drop the construction. |
| h.13 | Passive voice, missing subject: "Results are delivered." | Say who does it. "We deliver results in ten days." |
| h.14 | Em dashes. Also true in headlines and captions. | Comma, colon, or full stop. Rewrite so the pause is not needed. |
| h.23 | Filler: "in order to", "due to the fact that", "it is important to note", "has the ability to" | "To", "because", cut, "can". |
| h.24 | Stacked qualifiers: "could potentially", "may arguably help" | One qualifier or none. |
| h.26 | Hyphen pairs everywhere: *data-driven, end-to-end, best-in-class, client-facing, real-time* | Keep the hyphen before a noun; drop it after. Better: drop the phrase. |
| h.27 | False profundity: "The real question isn't X. It's Y." | Say the thing directly. |
| h.28 | Announcing the next point: "Let's explore how..." | Delete. The heading already announced it. |
| h.29 | Heading repeated as the first sentence under it | Start the paragraph with new information. |
| h.31 | Dramatic fragments as punchlines. Like this. Every time. | One is fine. Three in a row is a signature. |
| h.32 | Formulaic sayings: "at the end of the day", "the sky's the limit" | Cut. |
| h.33 | Fake candour: "Let's be honest.", "Look -" as an opener | Cut the opener, keep the sentence. |
| h.34 | Answering objections nobody raised | Cut, unless the client says buyers actually raise it. |
| h.35 | Rejecting fake alternatives: "This isn't about tools. It's about people." | Cut. |

## Formatting tells (h.15-h.19)

- Bold scattered through paragraphs for emphasis. Use it for one thing per screen, or none.
- Lists where every item opens with a bold mini-heading and a colon. Sometimes right; usually a
  symptom of an outline that never became prose.
- Title Case In Headings. Sentence case unless the client's brand mandates otherwise.
- Emojis. See the visual list, #3.
- Curly quotes are not a tell on their own - every CMS produces them. They count only stacked
  with other tells.

## What not to strip

From humanizer's false-positive list, adapted. Do not sand these out in the name of the rules:

- One em dash. One *however*. Curly quotes. None of these prove anything alone.
- Formal or technical vocabulary that is correct for the client's field. A structural engineer's
  page should sound like one.
- The client's own odd phrasing, in-jokes, regionalisms, and specific weird details. A real
  address, a real founding story, a sentence with mixed feelings in it. **These are the most
  valuable words on the page.** They are exactly what a generated page cannot produce.
- Varied sentence length. Generated prose settles into an even mid-length cadence; real writing
  does not. If every sentence in your draft is 14 to 20 words, that is the tell.
- Real disclaimers, scope statements, legal and safety notices, and genuine FAQ answers.

## The last check

Read the page aloud. Anything you would not say to the client's customer while standing in
front of them comes out.
